# Abstract

The Android operating system runs on billions of devices worldwide, making it a prime target for attackers. Over time, fundamental security mechanisms like SELinux and kernel sandboxing have improved significantly. As a result, attackers find it increasingly difficult to compromise standard apps or the kernel directly, and have shifted their focus to native system services. These services, particularly those operating within proprietary Hardware Abstraction Layers (HALs), serve as intermediaries between hardware and the OS. This positioning makes them ideal targets for privilege escalation.

Discovering vulnerabilities in these services using automated fuzz testing presents a significant challenge. The difficulty stems from the Inter-Process Communication (IPC) mechanisms they employ, such as `ioctl` and Binder. Traditional fuzzers that rely on random or slightly mutated byte generation are typically blocked during initial parsing, as they fail to construct the structured data formats expected by the service.

This seminar thesis examines the evolution of "interface-aware fuzzing" on Android, a methodology developed to overcome these parsing barriers. The narrative traces the progression from early static analysis methods to recent advancements in dynamic binary instrumentation. Specifically, the thesis analyzes three pivotal research systems: DIFUZE (which utilized static bitcode analysis for kernel `ioctl` interfaces), FANS (which applied AST extraction to map stateful Binder dependencies), and NASS, the primary focus of this work.

NASS addresses the "open-source blind spot" inherent in earlier tools. Because the most critical native services on modern commercial devices are closed-source binaries, tools like DIFUZE and FANS cannot analyze them. NASS circumvents this limitation by leveraging universal Remote Procedure Call (RPC) design principles to dynamically reverse-engineer interface definitions, a process termed Deserialization-Guided Interface Extraction (DGIE). Furthermore, NASS successfully isolates thread-specific coverage from concurrent, multi-threaded daemons utilizing Dynamic Binary Instrumentation (DBI).

By synthesizing these methodologies, this thesis demonstrates the critical role of interface awareness in fuzzing. Without such awareness, the discovery of deep memory-corruption vulnerabilities in the proprietary layers of modern mobile devices remains largely intractable.
